<section id="sec-tldr" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-tldr" id="sec-tldr">TL;DR</h2>
<p>MobileNetV4 (MNv4) is a family of image-classification backbones designed to sit on or near the accuracy/latency Pareto frontier on <strong>very different mobile hardware at once</strong>: phone CPUs, a Hexagon DSP, mobile GPUs, Apple’s Neural Engine, and Google’s EdgeTPU. The paper’s claim is “mostly Pareto optimal” across these, a deliberately hedged phrase.</p>
<p>Five ingredients produce the result:</p>
<ol type="1">
<li><strong>A hardware-independent way to reason about latency</strong>: a Roofline model swept over many <em>ridge points</em>, instead of counting MACs.</li>
<li><strong>Universal Inverted Bottleneck (UIB)</strong>: an inverted-bottleneck block with two <em>optional</em> depthwise convolutions. One searchable block unifies the classic IB, a ConvNeXt-style block, a ViT-style FFN, and a new “ExtraDW” variant.</li>
<li><strong>Mobile MQA</strong>: multi-query attention with asymmetric spatial downsampling of keys/values, plus an Einsum re-ordering that removes transposes. Reported attention-block speedups are 39% or more on accelerators.</li>
<li><strong>A refined NAS recipe</strong>: a two-stage TuNAS search, trained against an offline JFT-distillation dataset to reduce noise, for 750 epochs.</li>
<li><strong>A stronger distillation recipe</strong>: dynamic dataset mixing plus class-balanced JFT data. With it, MNv4-Hybrid-Large reaches <strong>87.0%</strong> ImageNet-1k top-1 (with additional JFT pretraining, see <a href="#sec-distill" class="quarto-xref">Section&nbsp;1.9</a>) at 3.8 ms on a Pixel 8 EdgeTPU.</li>
</ol>

<section id="sec-context" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-context" id="sec-context">Motivation and context</h2>
<p>On-device inference is attractive for latency, interactivity, and privacy, because data need not be streamed to a server. The difficulty is that a “mobile” model is deployed on a zoo of chips whose bottlenecks differ. A model hand-tuned for one phone CPU can be poor on an accelerator.</p>
<p>The paper situates itself among four lines of prior work:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mobile convnets</strong>: MobileNetV1 (depthwise-separable convs), V2 (inverted residuals and linear bottlenecks), GhostNet, MnasNet, MobileOne (re-parameterizable branches) <span class="citation" data-cites="howard2017mobilenets sandler2018mobilenetv2 vasu2023mobileone">[@howard2017mobilenets; @sandler2018mobilenetv2; @vasu2023mobileone]</span>.</li>
<li><strong>Efficient hybrids</strong> (conv + attention): MobileViT, MobileFormer, FastViT, which adds attention only in the last stage <span class="citation" data-cites="vasu2023fastvit">[@vasu2023fastvit]</span>.</li>
<li><strong>Efficient attention</strong>: linear-complexity approximations (EfficientViT), and downsampling Q/K/V or only K/V (EfficientFormerV2, CMT, NextViT).</li>
<li><strong>Hardware-aware NAS</strong>: NetAdapt, MnasNet, FBNet, MobileNetV3, MobileNet MultiHardware <span class="citation" data-cites="howard2019searching chu2021multihardware">[@howard2019searching; @chu2021multihardware]</span>, Once-for-All.</li>
</ul>
<p>What is new is the <em>combination</em> of a hardware-agnostic analysis tool, one flexible searchable block, an accelerator-friendly attention block, and a better search and distillation recipe.</p>
</section>
<section id="sec-roofline" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-roofline" id="sec-roofline">Hardware-independent efficiency: the Roofline view</h2>
<section id="why-macs-are-not-enough" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="why-macs-are-not-enough" id="why-macs-are-not-enough">Why MACs are not enough</h3>
<p>Many papers use MAC count as a latency proxy. That implicitly assumes memory is free. On accelerators, compute capability grows much faster than memory bandwidth, so many layers become <strong>memory-bound</strong>, and a model with fewer MACs can be slower.</p>
</section>
<section id="the-model" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="the-model" id="the-model">The model</h3>
<p>Following the classic Roofline model <span class="citation" data-cites="williams2009roofline">[@williams2009roofline]</span>, each layer <span class="math inline">\(i\)</span> has a compute time and a memory time, and the slower one determines its latency, because compute and memory overlap roughly in parallel:</p>
<p><span id="eq-roofline"><span class="math display">\[
\text{ModelTime}=\sum_i \max\big(\text{MACTime}_i,\ \text{MemTime}_i\big),\qquad
\text{MACTime}_i=\frac{\text{LayerMACs}_i}{\text{PeakMACs}},\quad
\text{MemTime}_i=\frac{\text{WeightBytes}_i+\text{ActivationBytes}_i}{\text{PeakMemBW}}
\tag{1}\]</span></span></p>
<p>A workload’s <strong>operational intensity</strong> (OI) is <span class="math inline">\(\text{LayerMACs}/(\text{WeightBytes}+\text{ActivationBytes})\)</span>. A hardware target is summarized by one number, its <strong>Ridge Point</strong>:</p>
<p><span class="math display">\[
\text{RP}=\frac{\text{PeakMACs}}{\text{PeakMemBW}}\quad[\text{MACs/byte}]
\]</span></p>
<p>A layer whose OI is below the RP is memory-bound on that hardware; above it, compute-bound.</p>
<div class="callout callout-style-default callout-tip callout-titled">
<div class="callout-header d-flex align-content-center">
<div class="callout-icon-container">
<i class="callout-icon"></i>
</div>
<div class="callout-title-container flex-fill">
<span class="screen-reader-only">Tip</span>Consequences the paper draws
</div>
</div>
<div class="callout-body-container callout-body">
<ul>
<li>Counting MACs is the special case <strong>RP = 0</strong> (infinite bandwidth, so every <span class="math inline">\(\text{MemTime}_i=0\)</span>).</li>
<li>Hardware with the same RP ranks networks the same way, so a sweep of RP from 0 to 500 MACs/byte covers future hardware too, as long as its RP falls in that range.</li>
<li>Low-RP hardware (CPUs) is mostly compute-bound; high-RP hardware (accelerators) is mostly memory-bound.</li>
</ul>
</div>
</div>
<p>The paper’s footnotes add two caveats: the model assumes software implementation does not matter (so tricks with complex memory access, like pruning, look better on paper than on device), and a similar framework was proposed independently by Lavin.</p>
</section>

<section id="what-mnv4s-design-takes-from-this" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="what-mnv4s-design-takes-from-this" id="what-mnv4s-design-takes-from-this">What MNv4’s design takes from this</h3>
<p>MNv4 puts <strong>expensive, MAC-heavy conv layers at the start</strong> of the network (they matter for capacity, and cost only at low RP) and keeps <strong>the same large final FC layers in every variant</strong> (they matter for accuracy, and cost only at high RP). Because the two costs hurt on opposite hardware classes, no single device pays both at once. The consequence is that smaller variants carry a relatively heavier FC cost on high-RP hardware.</p>

<section id="sec-uib" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-uib" id="sec-uib">Universal Inverted Bottleneck (UIB)</h2>
<section id="the-idea" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="the-idea" id="the-idea">The idea</h3>
<p>The MobileNetV2 inverted bottleneck (IB) is <span class="citation" data-cites="sandler2018mobilenetv2">[@sandler2018mobilenetv2]</span>: 1×1 pointwise (PW) <strong>expand</strong> → depthwise (DW) conv → 1×1 PW <strong>project</strong>. UIB makes one change: it adds an <strong>optional DW before the expansion</strong> and makes the <strong>middle DW optional too</strong>. Two on/off switches give four combinations, and NAS picks per block which to use.</p>

<td><strong>ConvNeXt-like</strong></td>
<td>✓</td>
<td>–</td>
<td>Cheaper spatial mixing, so larger kernels are affordable (mixing happens before expansion)</td>
</tr>
<tr class="odd">
<td><strong>ExtraDW</strong> (new)</td>
<td>✓</td>
<td>✓</td>
<td>Cheaply increases depth and receptive field; combines the benefits of ConvNeXt-like and IB</td>
</tr>
<tr class="even">
<td><strong>FFN</strong></td>
<td>–</td>
<td>–</td>
<td>Two pointwise convs with activation and norm: very accelerator-friendly, but best used alongside other blocks</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table>
</div>
</figure>
</div>
<p>The paper notes ExtraDW can be viewed as a MobileNetV1-style factorization of two standard convolutional blocks. At every stage UIB lets the network choose an ad-hoc spatial vs channel mixing trade-off, enlarge the receptive field where needed, and maximize compute utilization.</p>
</section>
<section id="why-this-stays-nas-friendly" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="why-this-stays-nas-friendly" id="why-this-stays-nas-friendly">Why this stays NAS-friendly</h3>
<p>The PW expansion and projection weights are <strong>shared</strong> across the candidate instantiations; only the DW ops are the searchable options. The paper reports that a SuperNet-based search then shares over 95% of the parameters between candidates, so the search space is manageable.</p>
</section>
<section id="fused-ib" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="fused-ib" id="fused-ib">Fused IB</h3>
<p>A <span class="math inline">\(k\times k\)</span> <strong>FusedIB</strong> replaces the expand-PW + DW pair with a single <span class="math inline">\(k\times k\)</span> full Conv2D followed by a 1×1 PW <span class="citation" data-cites="akin2022edgetpu">[@akin2022edgetpu]</span>. All MNv4 model <em>stems</em> use FusedIBs (Appendix D).</p>
</section>

<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="where-nas-actually-puts-each-block-appendix-d" id="where-nas-actually-puts-each-block-appendix-d">Where NAS actually puts each block (Appendix D)</h3>
<p>The authors summarize what TuNAS chose in the final Conv models:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>ExtraDW</strong> tends to appear at the <strong>start of each searchable stage</strong>, where resolution drops sharply. The two DWs enlarge the receptive field and soften the resolution loss. It is also common in early stages.</li>
<li><strong>FFN and ConvNeXt-like</strong> blocks show up in the <strong>final layers</strong>, where earlier layers have already mixed spatially and channel mixing gives more marginal gain.</li>
</ul>
</section>

<section id="sec-mqa" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-mqa" id="sec-mqa">Mobile MQA</h2>
<section id="background-mhsa-vs-mqa" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="background-mhsa-vs-mqa" id="background-mhsa-vs-mqa">Background: MHSA vs MQA</h3>
<p>Multi-head self-attention (MHSA) projects queries, keys, and values separately per head. <strong>Multi-query attention (MQA)</strong> <span class="citation" data-cites="shazeer2019mqa">[@shazeer2019mqa]</span> keeps multiple <em>query</em> heads but <strong>shares a single key head and a single value head</strong> across all of them. The paper cites language-model work showing that sharing one key/value head does not noticeably cost accuracy.</p>
</section>
<section id="why-mqa-suits-mobile-vision" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="why-mqa-suits-mobile-vision" id="why-mqa-suits-mobile-vision">Why MQA suits mobile vision</h3>
<p>The argument is again about operational intensity. When the number of tokens is small relative to the feature dimension, loading separate K/V projections per head is mostly memory traffic. Sharing them reduces bandwidth and raises operational intensity. In mobile hybrids this regime is typical, because attention is used only in the low-resolution, high-channel final stages and batch size is usually 1. To the authors’ knowledge this is the first use of MQA for mobile vision.</p>
</section>
<section id="asymmetric-spatial-downsampling" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="asymmetric-spatial-downsampling" id="asymmetric-spatial-downsampling">Asymmetric spatial downsampling</h3>
<p>Borrowing the “asymmetric” spirit of MQA, they add <strong>spatial reduction attention</strong> (SRA, from Pyramid Vision Transformer <span class="citation" data-cites="wang2021pvt">[@wang2021pvt]</span>) so keys and values live at lower resolution than queries. Two differences from PVT: the downsampling is a <strong>stride-2 3×3 depthwise conv</strong> instead of average pooling, which is cheap and adds capacity. The justification is that neighbouring tokens in hybrid models are already correlated thanks to early spatial-mixing convs.</p>
<p>The resulting block (the paper’s Eq. 2):</p>
<p><span id="eq-mqa"><span class="math display">\[
\text{MobileMQA}(X)=\text{Concat}(\text{attn}_1,\dots,\text{attn}_n)\,W^O,\qquad
\text{attn}_j=\text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{(XW^{Q}_j)\,(\text{SR}(X)W^{K})^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\text{SR}(X)W^{V}
\tag{2}\]</span></span></p>
<p>Here <span class="math inline">\(\text{SR}\)</span> is the stride-2 DW conv when spatial reduction is used, or the identity otherwise. Note <span class="math inline">\(W^K\)</span> and <span class="math inline">\(W^V\)</span> carry <strong>no head index</strong> (the sharing), while <span class="math inline">\(W^Q_j\)</span> does.</p>
</section>

<section id="the-einsum-optimization-appendix-g" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="the-einsum-optimization-appendix-g" id="the-einsum-optimization-appendix-g">The Einsum optimization (Appendix G)</h3>
<p>Even plain MQA, though faster than MHSA, was still about <strong>12× slower than UIB for the same MACs</strong>. The culprit was not arithmetic but <strong>transposes</strong>: on-device, an Einsum is lowered to transposes, reshapes, and batched matmuls. Transposes cost zero MACs but need a full read and write of the tensor, so they are pure memory traffic.</p>
<p>Two rules explain when transposes appear:</p>
<ol type="1">
<li><strong>Contracted and non-contracted indices are not contiguous</strong> in an input. A matmul needs the contracted indices adjacent so that reshaping to 2-D is free.</li>
<li><strong>Interleaved non-contracted indices or a reordered output</strong>: the output indices must come out in an order that avoids a post-matmul transpose.</li>
</ol>
<p>The fix is only a re-ordering of indices in the Einsum strings and in the weight tensor layout:</p>
<div class="code-copy-outer-scaffold"><div class="sourceCode" id="cb2"><pre class="sourceCode python code-with-copy"><code class="sourceCode python"><span id="cb2-1"><a href="#cb2-1" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1"></a><span class="co"># Slow: 2 transposes (non-contracted h, k interleaved with n; output reordered)</span></span>
<span id="cb2-2"><a href="#cb2-2" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1"></a>Q <span class="op">=</span> tf.einsum(<span class="st">"bnd,hdk-&gt;bhnk"</span>, X, P_q)</span>
<span id="cb2-3"><a href="#cb2-3" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1"></a><span class="co"># Fast: no transpose</span></span>
<span id="cb2-4"><a href="#cb2-4" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1"></a>Q <span class="op">=</span> tf.einsum(<span class="st">"bnd,dhk-&gt;bnhk"</span>, X, P_q)</span>
<span id="cb2-5"><a href="#cb2-5" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1"></a></span>
<span id="cb2-6"><a href="#cb2-6" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1"></a><span class="co"># Slow: 2 transposes (contracting h,v are not adjacent in the operands)</span></span>
<span id="cb2-7"><a href="#cb2-7" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1"></a>Y <span class="op">=</span> tf.einsum(<span class="st">"bhnv,hdv-&gt;bnd"</span>, O, P_o)</span>
<span id="cb2-8"><a href="#cb2-8" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1"></a><span class="co"># Fast: no transpose</span></span>
<span id="cb2-9"><a href="#cb2-9" aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1"></a>Y <span class="op">=</span> tf.einsum(<span class="st">"bnhv,hvd-&gt;bnd"</span>, O, P_o)</span></code></pre></div></div>
<p>(Fig. 11 of the paper writes the optimized output projection as <code>"bnhv,dhv-&gt;bnd"</code>, with the weight stored as <code>dhv</code>. That is the same idea as the <code>hvd</code> form in the Appendix G text: the contracted axes <code>h, v</code> are adjacent in both operands, so no transpose is needed. Treat the weight-axis order as a layout choice, not a fixed rule.)</p>

<section id="sec-design" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-design" id="sec-design">Designing the models</h2>
<section id="design-philosophy-simplicity" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="design-philosophy-simplicity" id="design-philosophy-simplicity">Design philosophy: simplicity</h3>
<p>Before searching, the authors ran correlation analyses between cost models and hardware, and kept only components whose costs correlate well across devices and that sit near the Pareto frontier. Findings that shaped the search space:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Multi-path blocks</strong> (group convs, ShuffleNet-style) have low MACs but are inefficient because of memory-access complexity.</li>
<li><strong>Modules with weak hardware support</strong>: Squeeze-and-Excite, GELU, and LayerNorm are poorly supported on DSPs (LayerNorm lags BatchNorm), and SE is slow on accelerators.</li>
<li><strong>Conventional components win</strong>: depthwise and pointwise convs, ReLU, BatchNorm, and plain attention.</li>
</ul>
<p>From these they derived three principles: use standard widely supported components, use flexible UIB blocks, and use straightforward attention (Mobile MQA).</p>
</section>
<section id="sec-nas" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-nas" id="sec-nas">Neural architecture search</h3>
<p>NAS uses <strong>TuNAS</strong> <span class="citation" data-cites="bender2020tunas">[@bender2020tunas]</span> with modifications, and a <strong>separate search per model size</strong> (rather than EfficientNet-style fixed scaling rules <span class="citation" data-cites="tan2019efficientnet">[@tan2019efficientnet]</span>).</p>

<section id="from-conv-to-hybrid-models" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="from-conv-to-hybrid-models" id="from-conv-to-hybrid-models">From Conv to Hybrid models</h3>
<p>Attention is most effective in the <strong>last stages</strong>, in line with other hybrids. MNv4-Hybrid models <strong>interleave Mobile MQA blocks with UIB blocks</strong> in the late stages. The architecture tables show the pattern: in Hybrid-M, MQA blocks alternate with ExtraDW/ConvNeXt/FFN blocks at the 16×16 stage and again at 8×8.</p>

<p><strong>What the authors conclude</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>CPU</strong>: roughly 2× faster than MobileNetV3, and well ahead of iso-accuracy competitors.</li>
<li><strong>EdgeTPU</strong>: as accurate as MobileNetV3 at about half the latency. MNv4-Conv-M is more than 50% faster than MobileOne-S4 and FastViT-S12, and 1.5 points above MobileNetV2 at comparable latency.</li>
<li><strong>S23 GPU and iPhone 13 (Apple Neural Engine)</strong>: mostly on the Pareto front. The nearest competitor on S23 GPU, EfficientViT, has more than 2× the CoreML latency at equal accuracy; FastViT, which is tuned for the Neural Engine, is second on CoreML but more than 5× slower on S23 GPU.</li>
<li><strong>DSP</strong>: Conv models remain top performers; hybrids are incompatible.</li>
<li>Some models reach the same accuracy with fewer MACs (EfficientViT is the example), but the paper’s argument is that MACs are the wrong target.</li>
</ul>

<ul class="nav nav-tabs" role="tablist"><li class="nav-item" role="presentation"><a class="nav-link active" id="tabset-1-1-tab" data-bs-toggle="tab" data-bs-target="#tabset-1-1" role="tab" aria-controls="tabset-1-1" aria-selected="true" href="">Who is bad where?</a></li><li class="nav-item" role="presentation"><a class="nav-link" id="tabset-1-2-tab" data-bs-toggle="tab" data-bs-target="#tabset-1-2" role="tab" aria-controls="tabset-1-2" aria-selected="false" href="">Why it matters</a></li></ul>
<div class="tab-content">
<div id="tabset-1-1" class="tab-pane active" role="tabpanel" aria-labelledby="tabset-1-1-tab">
<p>The paper’s point about <em>universality</em> is that most baselines are strong on one device class and weak on others:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>MobileNetV3</strong>: fine on CPU, poor on EdgeTPU, DSP, and GPU.</li>
<li><strong>FastViT</strong>: good on the Apple Neural Engine, poor on CPUs and GPUs.</li>
<li><strong>EfficientViT</strong>: good on GPUs, poor on the Neural Engine.</li>
<li><strong>MNv4-Conv</strong>: mostly Pareto-optimal on all of them.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div id="tabset-1-2" class="tab-pane" role="tabpanel" aria-labelledby="tabset-1-2-tab">
<p>If you ship one model to many phone SKUs, you cannot tune per device. A model that is merely <em>good</em> everywhere beats one that is <em>excellent</em> on one chip and bad on another.</p>
</div>

<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="benchmarking-methodology-appendix-b" id="benchmarking-methodology-appendix-b">Benchmarking methodology (Appendix B)</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Formats</strong>: TensorFlow Lite, <strong>INT8</strong> for mobile CPUs, Hexagon DSP, and EdgeTPUs; <strong>FP16</strong> for mobile GPUs.</li>
<li><strong>Protocol</strong>: about 1000 runs per measurement, mean taken; repeated 5 times; the <strong>median of the means</strong> is reported.</li>
<li><strong>CPU</strong>: pinned to the fastest core, XNNPACK backend.</li>
<li><strong>Apple Neural Engine</strong>: iPhone 13 (iOS 16.6.1), CoreMLTools 7.1, Xcode 15.0.1; PyTorch → CoreML MLProgram in Float16 with float16 MultiArray inputs to minimize input copying.</li>

<section id="sec-distill" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-distill" id="sec-distill">Enhanced distillation</h2>
<p>The baseline is the “Patient Teacher” recipe <span class="citation" data-cites="beyer2022patient">[@beyer2022patient]</span>: long training with consistent, strongly augmented inputs for teacher and student. MNv4 adds two techniques.</p>
<section id="dynamic-dataset-mixing" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="dynamic-dataset-mixing" id="dynamic-dataset-mixing">Dynamic dataset mixing</h3>
<p>Instead of one fixed augmentation pipeline, the student trains on a <strong>dynamic mixture of datasets</strong> produced with different augmentations:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>D1</strong>: Inception Crop + RandAugment (l2m9) on replicas of ImageNet-1k.</li>
<li><strong>D2</strong>: Inception Crop + <em>extreme</em> Mixup on replicas of ImageNet-1k (the Patient Teacher setup).</li>
<li><strong>D3</strong>: class-balanced in-domain data from <strong>JFT-300M</strong> <span class="citation" data-cites="sun2017jft">[@sun2017jft]</span>: resampled to 130K images per class (130 M total) using a relevance threshold above 0.3 from an ImageNet-1k-trained EfficientNet-B0 (following Noisy Student <span class="citation" data-cites="xie2020noisystudent">[@xie2020noisystudent]</span>); the top 130K for plentiful classes, replication for rare ones; the dataset is replicated 10×, with weaker augmentation (Inception Crop + RandAugment l2m5).</li>
</ul>
</section>

<section id="sec-critique" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-critique" id="sec-critique">A critical reading</h2>
<div class="callout callout-style-default callout-important callout-titled">
<div class="callout-header d-flex align-content-center">
<div class="callout-icon-container">
<i class="callout-icon"></i>
</div>
<div class="callout-title-container flex-fill">
<span class="screen-reader-only">Important</span>Where I would push back or verify
</div>
</div>
<div class="callout-body-container callout-body">
<ol type="1">
<li><strong>Mixed-recipe baselines.</strong> MobileNetV1–V3 were retrained with the improved recipe, but the other baselines use reported accuracies, so training-recipe differences are partly mixed into the architecture comparison. [Certain, from Sec. 7.1]</li>
<li><strong>“Mostly” Pareto-optimal.</strong> Hybrids and ConvNeXt are DSP-incompatible; EfficientViTs were not benchmarked on CPUs/EdgeTPU due to export limits; hybrids were excluded on CoreML. The “universal” claim is supported on the grid actually measured. [Certain, from the Fig. 1 caption]</li>
<li><strong>Single-source search target.</strong> The search optimized EdgeTPU latency; transfer to other devices relies on the correlation argument of Appendix A/F, validated across a handful of MobileNet-family models. Whether it holds for much different op sets is untested here. [Likely]</li>
<li><strong>No variance reported</strong> in the tables I read, so small gaps (for example UIB vs ConvNeXt at 0.1) are hard to interpret. [Certain for the extracted text; I did not see error bars]</li>
<li><strong>Headline 87%</strong> depends on JFT pretraining plus distillation from a 480 M-parameter EfficientNet-L2 teacher, so it is not directly comparable to ImageNet-only training numbers. The 83.4% figure is the IN-1k-only result.</li>
<li><strong>Roofline caveats.</strong> The model ignores software/kernel quality and cache effects; the paper itself says it over-credits access-pattern-heavy tricks like pruning.</li>
<li><strong>Internal inconsistencies</strong> noted in callouts: D1 replica count, the +0.6 vs +0.8 mixing gain, “filter sizes”, fixed expansion factor 4 vs the final tables, and the 9600-epoch Conv-S schedule.</li>
</ol>

<section id="sec-glossary" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-glossary" id="sec-glossary">Glossary</h2>
<dl>
<dt><strong>MAC</strong></dt>
<dd>
Multiply-accumulate operation; the usual unit of conv/matmul work.
</dd>
<dt><strong>Operational intensity (OI)</strong></dt>
<dd>
MACs per byte moved (weights + activations).
</dd>
<dt><strong>Ridge Point (RP)</strong></dt>
<dd>
PeakMACs / PeakMemBW of a device; the OI where a workload stops being memory-bound.
</dd>
<dt><strong>DW / PW</strong></dt>
<dd>
Depthwise / pointwise (1×1) convolution.
</dd>
<dt><strong>IB / FusedIB</strong></dt>
<dd>
Inverted bottleneck; its variant with a full <span class="math inline">\(k\times k\)</span> conv replacing the expand-PW + DW.
</dd>
<dt><strong>UIB / ExtraDW</strong></dt>
<dd>
Universal Inverted Bottleneck with two optional DWs; the variant with both DWs present.
</dd>
<dt><strong>MHSA / MQA</strong></dt>
<dd>
Multi-head self-attention; multi-query attention (shared K and V heads).
</dd>
<dt><strong>SRA</strong></dt>
<dd>
Spatial-reduction attention: downsampling K and V before attention.
</dd>
<dt><strong>TuNAS</strong></dt>
<dd>
Weight-sharing NAS with an RL-style controller and a hardware-cost-aware reward.
</dd>
<dt><strong>Pareto optimal</strong></dt>
<dd>
No other model is both more accurate and faster.
</dd>
</dl>
