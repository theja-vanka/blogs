<section id="sec-tldr" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-tldr" id="sec-tldr">TL;DR</h2>
<p>3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) is a method for <strong>novel-view synthesis</strong>: given photos of a static scene plus the camera poses recovered by Structure-from-Motion, it builds a 3D representation you can render from any new viewpoint. Its headline achievement is being the first method to deliver <strong>state-of-the-art visual quality <em>and</em> real-time rendering (≥ 30 fps, in practice 100+ fps) at 1080p</strong> for unbounded, complete real-world scenes, while training in tens of minutes rather than hours or days.</p>
<p>It replaces the neural network at the heart of NeRF with an <strong>explicit cloud of millions of anisotropic 3D Gaussians</strong> — differentiable, semi-transparent “blobs,” each with a position, a shape (covariance), an opacity, and a view-dependent color. Three pillars:</p>
<ol type="1">
<li><strong>A 3D Gaussian scene representation</strong> that keeps the continuous/differentiable properties of volumetric radiance fields while being explicit enough to rasterize extremely fast, wasting no computation on empty space.</li>
<li><strong>Optimization with adaptive density control</strong> — gradient descent on the Gaussians’ parameters, interleaved with clone/split/prune steps that grow the right level of detail where needed.</li>
<li><strong>A fast, differentiable, tile-based rasterizer</strong> that sorts all splats once per frame with a GPU radix sort, respects visibility order via α-blending, and back-propagates to an unlimited number of Gaussians per pixel.</li>
</ol>
<p>The result equals or beats Mip-NeRF360 (previous quality SOTA: ~48 GPU-hours to train, ~10 s/frame to render) while training in 35–45 minutes and rendering in real time.</p>
</section>
<section id="sec-intro" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-intro" id="sec-intro">Introduction — The Problem and the Idea</h2>
<section id="the-tension-the-paper-resolves" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="the-tension-the-paper-resolves" id="the-tension-the-paper-resolves">The tension the paper resolves</h3>
<p>By 2023 novel-view synthesis had split into two camps, each forced to give something up:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Continuous / neural representations (NeRF and descendants).</strong> Optimize an MLP queried by volumetric ray-marching. Continuity aids optimization and gives beautiful results, but rendering requires <strong>stochastic sampling of many points per ray</strong> — slow and noisy. The quality leader, <strong>Mip-NeRF360</strong>, needed up to <strong>48 hours</strong> of training and rendered at seconds per frame.</li>
<li><strong>Fast grid / hash methods (InstantNGP, Plenoxels).</strong> Storing interpolatable features in voxel/hash grids cut training to minutes and reached <em>interactive</em> (~10–15 fps) rates, but they still ray-march, still struggle with empty space, and their quality is capped by the structured acceleration grid.</li>
</ul>
<p>Nobody could get <strong>all three</strong> at once — SOTA quality, fast training, <em>and</em> real-time 1080p rendering of full scenes. That is the gap 3DGS closes.</p>
</section>
<section id="the-core-insight" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="the-core-insight" id="the-core-insight">The core insight</h3>
<p>Meshes and points map cleanly onto fast GPU rasterization. NeRFs win on quality because volumetric α-blending is a forgiving, gradient-friendly image-formation model. 3DGS asks: <em>what primitive is simultaneously (a) a differentiable volumetric element and (b) trivially and cheaply rasterizable?</em></p>
<p>The answer is an <strong>anisotropic 3D Gaussian</strong> — a smooth differentiable volumetric bump (so optimization behaves like a radiance field) that projects to a 2D Gaussian “splat” blendable with ordinary α-compositing (so rendering behaves like point rasterization). Empty space contains no Gaussians, so no compute is wasted there.</p>
</section>
<section id="the-three-contributions" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="the-three-contributions" id="the-three-contributions">The three contributions</h3>
<ol type="1">
<li><strong>Anisotropic 3D Gaussians</strong> as a high-quality, unstructured representation of radiance fields.</li>
<li>An <strong>optimization method</strong> for Gaussian properties, interleaved with <strong>adaptive density control</strong>.</li>
<li>A <strong>fast, differentiable, visibility-aware GPU renderer</strong> supporting anisotropic splatting and efficient backpropagation.</li>
</ol>
<p>Input is the same as NeRF’s: SfM-calibrated cameras, which produce a <strong>sparse point cloud for free</strong>. Unlike most prior point-based methods, 3DGS needs <strong>only SfM points</strong> — no Multi-View Stereo. On synthetic data it even works from <strong>random initialization</strong>. Converged scenes hold <strong>1–5 million Gaussians</strong>.</p>
</section>
</section>
<section id="sec-related" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-related" id="sec-related">Related Work — Where 3DGS Sits</h2>
<section id="traditional-reconstruction-and-rendering" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="traditional-reconstruction-and-rendering" id="traditional-reconstruction-and-rendering">Traditional reconstruction and rendering</h3>
<p>Early novel-view synthesis used <strong>light fields</strong>. <strong>Structure-from-Motion (SfM)</strong> enabled turning photo collections into novel views, producing a sparse point cloud as a by-product. <strong>Multi-View Stereo (MVS)</strong> gave full 3D reconstruction feeding image-based rendering methods that re-project and blend inputs — but these fail on unreconstructed regions and MVS “over-reconstruction” (invented geometry).</p>
</section>
<section id="neural-rendering-and-radiance-fields" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="neural-rendering-and-radiance-fields" id="neural-rendering-and-radiance-fields">Neural rendering and radiance fields</h3>
<p><strong>NeRF</strong> introduced positional encoding and importance sampling with a large MLP — great quality, slow speed. The quality SOTA became <strong>Mip-NeRF360</strong>. Acceleration methods exploit three levers: spatial data structures for features, cheaper encodings, and smaller/absent MLPs:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>InstantNGP</strong> — multiresolution <strong>hash grid</strong> + occupancy grid + small MLP.</li>
<li><strong>Plenoxels</strong> — sparse <strong>voxel grid</strong> interpolating a continuous density field, <em>no neural network at all</em>.</li>
</ul>
<p>Both use <strong>spherical harmonics</strong> for directional appearance; both struggle with empty space and are limited by structured grids.</p>
</section>
<section id="point-based-rendering-and-the-shared-image-formation-model" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="point-based-rendering-and-the-shared-image-formation-model" id="point-based-rendering-and-the-shared-image-formation-model">Point-based rendering and the shared image-formation model</h3>
<p>Naïve point rendering has holes and aliasing, fixed by <strong>“splatting”</strong> primitives larger than a pixel (the EWA lineage of Zwicker et al.). The key theoretical point: point-based α-blending and NeRF volumetric rendering share <em>the same image-formation model</em>.</p>
<p>Volumetric rendering along a ray:</p>
<p><span id="eq-vol"><span class="math display">\[
C = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i\,\bigl(1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)\bigr)\,\mathbf{c}_i,
\qquad T_i = \exp\!\left(-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\sigma_j \delta_j\right).
\tag{1}\]</span></span></p>
<p>Re-writing with <span class="math inline">\(\alpha_i = 1 - \exp(-\sigma_i \delta_i)\)</span>:</p>
<p><span id="eq-vol2"><span class="math display">\[
C = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i\,\alpha_i\,\mathbf{c}_i,
\qquad T_i = \prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-\alpha_j).
\tag{2}\]</span></span></p>
<p>A typical neural point method blends <span class="math inline">\(N\)</span> ordered points overlapping a pixel:</p>
<p><span id="eq-point"><span class="math display">\[
C = \sum_{i \in N} \mathbf{c}_i\,\alpha_i \prod_{j=1}^{i-1}(1-\alpha_j),
\tag{3}\]</span></span></p>
<p>where <span class="math inline">\(\alpha_i\)</span> evaluates a <strong>2D Gaussian with covariance <span class="math inline">\(\Sigma\)</span></strong> times a learned per-point opacity. <a href="#eq-vol2" class="quarto-xref">Equation&nbsp;2</a> and <a href="#eq-point" class="quarto-xref">Equation&nbsp;3</a> are the <strong>same model</strong>; the difference is algorithmic — NeRF implicitly represents space and pays for random sampling, while points are explicit, unstructured, and editable.</p>
<p><strong>Pulsar</strong> inspired the tile-based sorted renderer, but is order-<em>independent</em>. 3DGS keeps <strong>approximate conventional α-blending on sorted splats</strong> (visibility respected), back-propagates to <strong>all</strong> splats in a pixel, and rasterizes <strong>anisotropic</strong> splats — the ingredients behind its quality. Prior 3D-Gaussian uses targeted single isolated objects; 3DGS scales to <strong>complete, complex, high-depth-complexity scenes</strong>, indoor and outdoor.</p>
</section>
</section>

<section id="sec-repr" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-repr" id="sec-repr">Differentiable 3D Gaussian Splatting — The Representation</h2>
<section id="why-gaussians-and-how-they-are-defined" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="why-gaussians-and-how-they-are-defined" id="why-gaussians-and-how-they-are-defined">Why Gaussians, and how they are defined</h3>
<p>The goal: a primitive that inherits differentiable-volumetric properties yet is unstructured and explicit for fast rendering — and that works from <strong>normal-free</strong> SfM points. 3D Gaussians fit. A Gaussian is defined by a full 3D covariance <span class="math inline">\(\Sigma\)</span> in <strong>world space</strong>, centered at mean <span class="math inline">\(\mu\)</span>:</p>
<p><span id="eq-gaussian"><span class="math display">\[
G(x) = e^{-\frac{1}{2}(x-\mu)^{T}\,\Sigma^{-1}\,(x-\mu)}.
\tag{4}\]</span></span></p>
<p>In blending, <span class="math inline">\(G(x)\)</span> is multiplied by the Gaussian’s opacity <span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>.</p>
</section>
<section id="projecting-3d-gaussians-to-2d-ewa-splatting" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="projecting-3d-gaussians-to-2d-ewa-splatting" id="projecting-3d-gaussians-to-2d-ewa-splatting">Projecting 3D Gaussians to 2D (EWA splatting)</h3>
<p>For rendering, each Gaussian becomes a 2D splat. Following Zwicker et al.’s EWA framework, given a viewing transform <span class="math inline">\(W\)</span>, the camera-space covariance is:</p>
<p><span id="eq-project"><span class="math display">\[
\Sigma' = J\,W\,\Sigma\,W^{T}\,J^{T},
\tag{5}\]</span></span></p>
<p>where <span class="math inline">\(J\)</span> is the <strong>Jacobian of the affine approximation</strong> of the projective transform. Dropping the third row and column of <span class="math inline">\(\Sigma'\)</span> yields a <span class="math inline">\(2\times2\)</span> covariance — a proper 2D Gaussian splat.</p>
</section>
<section id="a-parameterization-that-stays-valid-under-gradient-descent" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="a-parameterization-that-stays-valid-under-gradient-descent" id="a-parameterization-that-stays-valid-under-gradient-descent">A parameterization that stays valid under gradient descent</h3>
<p>You cannot optimize the raw entries of <span class="math inline">\(\Sigma\)</span> directly: a covariance is only physically meaningful when <strong>positive semi-definite</strong>, and unconstrained gradient steps produce invalid matrices. Since <span class="math inline">\(\Sigma\)</span> describes an <strong>ellipsoid</strong>, factor it into a rotation and a scale:</p>
<p><span id="eq-cov"><span class="math display">\[
\Sigma = R\,S\,S^{T}\,R^{T}.
\tag{6}\]</span></span></p>
<p>Stored <strong>separately and optimized independently</strong>: a <strong>3D scale vector</strong> <span class="math inline">\(s\)</span> (→ diagonal <span class="math inline">\(S\)</span>) and a <strong>quaternion</strong> <span class="math inline">\(q\)</span> (normalized → rotation <span class="math inline">\(R\)</span>). As expressive as a raw covariance, but <em>always valid</em>. To avoid autodiff overhead on this hot path, all gradients are <strong>derived explicitly</strong> (<a href="#sec-gradients" class="quarto-xref">Section&nbsp;1.12</a>).</p>
<div class="callout callout-style-default callout-tip callout-titled">
<div class="callout-header d-flex align-content-center">
<div class="callout-icon-container">
<i class="callout-icon"></i>
</div>
<div class="callout-title-container flex-fill">
<span class="screen-reader-only">Tip</span>The payoff
</div>
</div>
<div class="callout-body-container callout-body">
<p>Anisotropic Gaussians can hug real surfaces — large flat blobs for big homogeneous regions, thin elongated ones for fine structures — giving a compact yet accurate representation (the paper’s Fig. 3 shrinks Gaussians by 60% to expose their anisotropy; see the <a href="https://repo-sam.inria.fr/fungraph/3d-gaussian-splatting/">project page</a>).</p>
</div>

<section id="sec-opt" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-opt" id="sec-opt">Optimization with Adaptive Density Control</h2>
<section id="the-optimization-step" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="the-optimization-step" id="the-optimization-step">The optimization step</h3>
<p>Iterate: render a training view, compare to the captured image, back-propagate, step. Because 3D→2D projection is ambiguous, the optimizer must <strong>create, destroy, and move</strong> geometry. Key choices:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Optimizer:</strong> Adam SGD, with custom CUDA kernels on the critical path (rasterization is the bottleneck).</li>
<li><strong>Activations:</strong> <strong>sigmoid</strong> on <span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span> (keeps it in <span class="math inline">\([0,1)\)</span> with smooth gradients); <strong>exponential</strong> on covariance scale.</li>
<li><strong>Initial covariance:</strong> isotropic, axis length = mean distance to the <strong>3 nearest</strong> SfM points.</li>
<li><strong>LR schedule:</strong> exponential decay (à la Plenoxels), applied to <strong>positions only</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Loss:</strong> <span class="math inline">\(\mathcal{L}_1\)</span> + <strong>D-SSIM</strong>:</li>
</ul>
<p><span id="eq-loss"><span class="math display">\[
\mathcal{L} = (1-\lambda)\,\mathcal{L}_1 + \lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{D-SSIM}},
\qquad \lambda = 0.2.
\tag{7}\]</span></span></p>
</section>
<section id="adaptive-control-of-gaussians-clone-split-prune" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="adaptive-control-of-gaussians-clone-split-prune" id="adaptive-control-of-gaussians-clone-split-prune">Adaptive control of Gaussians — clone, split, prune</h3>
<p>Every <strong>100 iterations</strong> (after warm-up), density is adjusted. Two failure modes are targeted, and <strong>both show up as large view-space positional gradients</strong> (the optimizer straining to move Gaussians):</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Under-reconstruction</strong> — missing geometry; small Gaussians can’t cover a region.</li>
<li><strong>Over-reconstruction</strong> — one big Gaussian sprawling over detailed geometry.</li>
</ul>
<p>Gaussians with average view-space position-gradient magnitude above <span class="math inline">\(\tau_{pos} = 0.0002\)</span> are densified:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Under-reconstruction → CLONE:</strong> copy the small Gaussian, shift the copy along the positional gradient. <em>Adds volume and a Gaussian.</em></li>
<li><strong>Over-reconstruction → SPLIT:</strong> replace the large Gaussian with <strong>two</strong>, dividing scale by <span class="math inline">\(\phi = 1.6\)</span>, sampling positions using the original as a PDF. <em>Conserves volume, adds a Gaussian.</em></li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Pruning / count control.</strong> Remove Gaussians with <span class="math inline">\(\alpha &lt; \epsilon_\alpha\)</span>. To fight near-camera “floaters,” every <strong>N = 3000</strong> iterations <span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span> is <strong>reset near zero</strong>; the optimizer re-raises <span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span> only where needed, and culling removes the rest. Oversized world-space or viewspace Gaussians are periodically removed. Gaussians <strong>stay Euclidean primitives at all times</strong> — no space warping/contraction (unlike Mip-NeRF360 or Plenoxels).</p>

<section id="sec-raster" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-raster" id="sec-raster">Fast Differentiable Rasterizer for Gaussians</h2>
<p>Design goals: fast rendering; fast sorting for approximate α-blending (including anisotropic splats); and <strong>no hard cap</strong> on how many splats can receive gradients (a Pulsar limitation).</p>
<section id="tile-based-forward-pass" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="tile-based-forward-pass" id="tile-based-forward-pass">Tile-based forward pass</h3>
<ol type="1">
<li><strong>Tile the screen</strong> into <strong>16×16 pixel tiles</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Cull</strong> against the view frustum — keep Gaussians whose <strong>99% confidence ellipsoid</strong> intersects it; a <strong>guard band</strong> trivially rejects unstable extreme-position Gaussians.</li>
<li><strong>Instantiate</strong> each Gaussian once per <strong>overlapped tile</strong>, with a <strong>64-bit key</strong>: low 32 bits = view-space depth, high bits = tile ID.</li>
<li><strong>Sort</strong> all instances with a <strong>single GPU radix sort</strong>. Whole-image depth order resolved at once — <strong>no per-pixel sorting</strong>. Blending is therefore <em>approximate</em>, but error vanishes as splats approach pixel size.</li>
<li><strong>Build per-tile ranges</strong> (one thread per key, comparing high bits with neighbors).</li>
<li><strong>Rasterize</strong>: one thread block per tile; threads <strong>collaboratively load</strong> Gaussians into shared memory; each pixel walks its list <strong>front-to-back</strong>, accumulating color and <span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>, stopping when <span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span> saturates; the tile terminates when all pixels saturate.</li>
</ol>
</section>
<section id="backward-pass-without-storing-per-pixel-lists" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="backward-pass-without-storing-per-pixel-lists" id="backward-pass-without-storing-per-pixel-lists">Backward pass without storing per-pixel lists</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Reuse</strong> the sorted array and per-tile ranges, traversing <strong>back-to-front</strong>.</li>
<li>A pixel processes a point only if its depth ≤ the depth of the <strong>last point that contributed</strong> in the forward pass.</li>
<li>Instead of storing every intermediate opacity, each pixel stores only the <strong>total accumulated <span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span></strong>, and the backward pass <strong>divides</strong> by each point’s <span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span> to recover the coefficients.</li>
</ul>
<p>Because the only stopping criterion is <span class="math inline">\(\alpha\)</span>-saturation (not a fixed count), the method handles <strong>arbitrary depth complexity</strong> with no scene-specific tuning — critical to quality (the ablation capping gradient depth to 10 splats lost ~11 dB PSNR).</p>

<section id="numerical-stability" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="numerical-stability" id="numerical-stability">Numerical stability</h3>
<p>Reconstructing opacities by division risks divide-by-zero. Safeguards: skip blending with <span class="math inline">\(\alpha &lt; \epsilon\)</span> (<span class="math inline">\(\epsilon = \tfrac{1}{255}\)</span>), clamp <span class="math inline">\(\alpha \le 0.99\)</span>, and — before adding a Gaussian — stop front-to-back blending if accumulated opacity would exceed <strong>0.9999</strong>.</p>
</section>

<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="implementation-details" id="implementation-details">Implementation details</h3>

<section id="ablations-table-3" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="ablations-table-3" id="ablations-table-3">Ablations (Table 3)</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Initialization from SfM.</strong> Random init still avoids total failure but <strong>degrades in the background</strong> and leaves un-removable floaters (Fig. 7).</li>
<li><strong>Densification: clone vs.&nbsp;split.</strong> <strong>Split</strong> helps <strong>background</strong> reconstruction; <strong>clone</strong> aids <strong>thin structures</strong> and convergence (Fig. 8).</li>
<li><strong>Unlimited gradient depth.</strong> Capping gradients to <strong>N = 10</strong> front-most splats caused <strong>unstable optimization</strong> and <strong>−11 dB PSNR</strong> on Truck (Fig. 9).</li>
<li><strong>Anisotropic covariance.</strong> Replacing full covariance with a single isotropic radius <strong>significantly lowers quality</strong> (Fig. 10).</li>
<li><strong>Spherical harmonics.</strong> SH improves PSNR by modeling view-dependent effects.</li>
</ul>
</section>
<section id="limitations" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="limitations" id="limitations">Limitations</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Artifacts in poorly observed regions</strong> (elongated/“splotchy” Gaussians) — Mip-NeRF360 also struggles (Figs. 11, 12).</li>
<li><strong>Popping artifacts</strong> for large Gaussians, aggravated by guard-band rejection and simple global-sort visibility; anti-aliasing and principled culling are future work.</li>
<li><strong>No regularization</strong> yet.</li>
<li><strong>Memory:</strong> peak training memory can exceed <strong>20 GB</strong> (unoptimized prototype); models are hundreds of MB + 30–500 MB rasterizer overhead.</li>
<li><strong>Very large scenes</strong> may need a <strong>lower position learning rate</strong>.</li>
</ul>
</section>
</section>
<section id="sec-conclusion" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-conclusion" id="sec-conclusion">Discussion and Conclusions</h2>
<p>3DGS is the <strong>first method to deliver truly real-time, high-quality radiance-field rendering</strong> across diverse scenes and capture styles, with training competitive with the fastest prior methods. Central lesson: <strong>a continuous representation is <em>not</em> strictly necessary</strong> for fast, high-quality radiance-field training — an explicit 3D-Gaussian primitive preserves the volumetric properties that make optimization work while enabling direct, fast, splat-based rasterization.</p>
<p>~<strong>80% of training time is still Python/PyTorch</strong> (only rasterization is CUDA); porting the rest to CUDA should yield large further speedups. <strong>Mesh reconstruction from Gaussians</strong> is flagged as intriguing future work.</p>
</section>
<section id="sec-intuition" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-intuition" id="sec-intuition">Intuition Cheat-Sheet</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>A Gaussian = a fuzzy, colored, semi-transparent ellipsoidal blob.</strong> Millions of them, overlapping, <em>are</em> the scene.</li>
<li><strong>Training = sculpting a cloud of blobs</strong> so that, α-blended from every training camera, they reproduce the photos.</li>
<li><strong>Density control = automatic level-of-detail.</strong> Missing detail → clone; over-worked blob → split; transparent/oversized → prune.</li>
<li><strong>Rendering = sort once, blend front-to-back per 16×16 tile.</strong> No ray-marching, no network in the loop, no wasted work in empty space — hence 100+ fps.</li>
<li><strong>Why it beats NeRF on speed:</strong> NeRF queries a network at many random samples per ray; 3DGS rasterizes explicit primitives the GPU loves, sharing the same α-blending math, so quality need not suffer.</li>
</ul>
</section>

<section id="sec-gradients" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-gradients" id="sec-gradients">Appendix A — Explicit Gradient Derivation</h2>
<p>Autodiff is avoided on the hot path; gradients are derived by hand. With <span class="math inline">\(\Sigma / \Sigma'\)</span> the world/view covariances, <span class="math inline">\(q\)</span> rotation, <span class="math inline">\(s\)</span> scale, <span class="math inline">\(W\)</span> view transform, <span class="math inline">\(J\)</span> the projective-affine Jacobian:</p>
<p><span id="eq-chain"><span class="math display">\[
\frac{d\Sigma'}{ds} = \frac{d\Sigma'}{d\Sigma}\frac{d\Sigma}{ds},
\qquad
\frac{d\Sigma'}{dq} = \frac{d\Sigma'}{d\Sigma}\frac{d\Sigma}{dq}.
\tag{8}\]</span></span></p>
<p>Let <span class="math inline">\(U = JW\)</span>, with <span class="math inline">\(\Sigma'\)</span> the symmetric upper-left <span class="math inline">\(2\times2\)</span> block of <span class="math inline">\(U\Sigma U^T\)</span>. Then</p>
<p><span class="math display">\[
\frac{\partial \Sigma'}{\partial \Sigma_{ij}} =
\begin{pmatrix} U_{1,i}U_{1,j} &amp; U_{1,i}U_{2,j} \\ U_{1,j}U_{2,i} &amp; U_{2,i}U_{2,j} \end{pmatrix}.
\]</span></p>
<p>Writing <span class="math inline">\(M = RS\)</span> so <span class="math inline">\(\Sigma = MM^T\)</span>, the shared factor is <span class="math inline">\(\dfrac{d\Sigma}{dM} = 2M^T\)</span>. For scale, <span class="math inline">\(\dfrac{\partial M_{i,j}}{\partial s_k} = R_{i,k}\)</span> if <span class="math inline">\(j=k\)</span>, else <span class="math inline">\(0\)</span>. Rotation derivatives follow from the unit-quaternion-to-matrix map <span class="math inline">\(R(q)\)</span>, yielding closed forms for <span class="math inline">\(\partial M / \partial q_r, \partial q_i, \partial q_j, \partial q_k\)</span>. Quaternion-normalization gradients are straightforward. <strong>Takeaway:</strong> every parameter has an explicit gradient, so the CUDA kernels avoid autodiff overhead entirely.</p>
</section>
