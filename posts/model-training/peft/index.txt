<section id="why-this-survey-exists" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="why-this-survey-exists" id="why-this-survey-exists">Why This Survey Exists</h2>
<p>Transformer-based pretrained language models (PLMs) — BERT, RoBERTa, T5, GPT-style models, and now large language models (LLMs) like LLaMA and Falcon — have become the default starting point for almost any NLP task. The standard recipe has always been: pretrain once on a huge unlabeled corpus, then <strong>fully fine-tune</strong> (update every single parameter) on your downstream task.</p>
<p>That recipe is breaking down under its own weight. The paper opens with a blunt illustration of the problem: model size has exploded from BERT’s 110 million parameters to Falcon’s 180 billion, and “to perform task-specific full fine-tuning with Falcon-180B, a minimum of 5120GB of computational resources may be required.” That’s not a typo — it’s roughly 5 terabytes of GPU memory for one fine-tuning run. Very few labs, let alone individuals, have that kind of hardware.</p>
<p><strong>Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)</strong> is the family of techniques built to solve exactly this problem. As the authors put it, PEFT “involves employing various deep learning techniques to reduce the number of trainable parameters while still maintaining comparable performance to the full fine-tuning.” Instead of updating all ~100M–100B+ parameters, PEFT methods freeze almost everything and train only a small add-on (sometimes well under 1% of the total parameter count), while trying to match — or in some cases beat — full fine-tuning’s downstream accuracy.</p>
<p>Beyond just saving compute, the paper notes PEFT has a few side benefits worth calling out explicitly:</p>
<ul>
<li>Freezing most of the pretrained weights <strong>preserves the general knowledge</strong> the model learned during pretraining.</li>
<li>It <strong>reduces catastrophic forgetting</strong> — the tendency of a fully fine-tuned model to “unlearn” broadly useful capabilities while specializing on a narrow task.</li>
<li>It <strong>mitigates overfitting</strong>, which matters a lot when your downstream dataset is small relative to the model’s capacity.</li>
</ul>
<p>The authors argue that although several PEFT surveys already existed by the time they wrote this one, those surveys had gaps — incomplete taxonomies, missing comparative experiments, or narrow scope. So this paper sets out to do four things comprehensively:</p>
<ol type="1">
<li>Propose a clean, five-way <strong>taxonomy</strong> of PEFT methods: <em>additive</em>, <em>partial</em>, <em>reparameterized</em>, <em>hybrid</em>, and <em>unified</em> fine-tuning.</li>
<li>Walk through the mechanics of dozens of specific PEFT methods within that taxonomy.</li>
<li>Run <strong>actual experiments</strong> with 11 representative PEFT methods across natural language understanding, machine translation, and instruction-tuned generation, measuring both task performance <em>and</em> resource efficiency (parameters + memory).</li>
<li>Survey PEFT’s use in <strong>applications</strong> beyond plain task fine-tuning (multi-task learning, cross-lingual transfer, and even backdoor attacks/defenses), and lay out open research directions.</li>
</ol>
<p>This guide follows that same structure, in the same order, expanding the paper’s dense IEEE-survey prose with analogies, worked-through equations, and comparison tables.</p>
</section>
<section id="background-transformers-and-full-fine-tuning" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="background-transformers-and-full-fine-tuning" id="background-transformers-and-full-fine-tuning">Background: Transformers and Full Fine-Tuning</h2>
<section id="the-transformer-in-the-vocabulary-peft-papers-use" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="the-transformer-in-the-vocabulary-peft-papers-use" id="the-transformer-in-the-vocabulary-peft-papers-use">The Transformer, in the vocabulary PEFT papers use</h3>
<p>The Transformer “has emerged as a foundational architecture for numerous PLMs; it adopts an encoder-decoder architecture, comprised of a stack of encoder and decoder layers, each equipped with the self-attention mechanism.” Every PEFT method in this survey inserts itself into, or modifies, one or more of the following pieces:</p>
<p><strong>Step 1 — Project the input into queries, keys, and values.</strong> Given an input representation <span class="math inline">\(X\)</span>, three learned linear projections produce:</p>
<p><span class="math display">\[K = XW_k + b_k,\qquad Q = XW_q + b_q,\qquad V = XW_v + b_v\]</span></p>
<p><strong>Step 2 — Scaled dot-product self-attention.</strong> The queries and keys determine how much each token “attends to” every other token, and that attention pattern is used to mix the values:</p>
<p><span class="math display">\[\mathrm{Attn}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{Softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\right) V\]</span></p>
<p><strong>Step 3 — Multi-head attention.</strong> Rather than doing this once, the model does it <span class="math inline">\(h\)</span> times in parallel with different learned projections per “head,” then concatenates and projects the result:</p>
<p><span class="math display">\[\mathrm{MHA}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{Concat}(\mathrm{head}_1, \dots, \mathrm{head}_h) W^O\]</span> <span class="math display">\[\mathrm{head}_i = \mathrm{Attn}(QW_i^Q, KW_i^K, VW_i^V)\]</span></p>
<p><strong>Step 4 — Feed-forward network (FFN).</strong> After attention, each position is passed independently through a two-layer MLP with a nonlinearity in between:</p>
<p><span class="math display">\[\mathrm{FFN}(X) = \mathrm{ReLU}(XW_1 + b_1)W_2 + b_2\]</span></p>
<p>These blocks are wrapped with residual (skip) connections and layer normalization. Keep this picture in mind — attention block, FFN block, residual connections, layer norm — because almost every PEFT method in this survey can be located on it: adapters are new little modules squeezed in after attention and/or FFN; prefix-tuning modifies the <span class="math inline">\(K\)</span>/<span class="math inline">\(V\)</span> that attention consumes; LoRA modifies the weight matrices (<span class="math inline">\(W_q\)</span>, <span class="math inline">\(W_k\)</span>, <span class="math inline">\(W_v\)</span>, <span class="math inline">\(W_1\)</span>, <span class="math inline">\(W_2\)</span>, …) themselves via a low-rank side-path; bias-tuning modifies only the <span class="math inline">\(b\)</span> terms; and so on.</p>
</section>
<section id="full-fine-tuning-the-expensive-baseline" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="full-fine-tuning-the-expensive-baseline" id="full-fine-tuning-the-expensive-baseline">Full fine-tuning: the expensive baseline</h3>
<p>“Full fine-tuning of transformer-based PLMs involves training the entire model, including all layers and parameters, on a specific downstream task using task-specific data.” Concretely: you initialize from the pretrained weights, then run ordinary backpropagation and gradient descent on your labeled downstream dataset, updating <em>every</em> weight in the network.</p>
<p>Two problems, both already mentioned above but worth stating plainly here because the rest of the paper is essentially a 20-page answer to them:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Resource cost.</strong> Full fine-tuning “necessitates substantial computational resources and labeled data” — you need to store gradients and optimizer states (e.g., Adam’s two moment buffers) for every parameter, which for a modern LLM multiplies the raw parameter count’s memory footprint several times over.</li>
<li><strong>Overfitting risk.</strong> Full fine-tuning “may give rise to overfitting when the task-specific dataset is small,” since you’re letting a massive number of degrees of freedom adapt to what might be a comparatively tiny labeled set.</li>
</ul>
<p>PEFT is the response: freeze (almost) everything, train a small, cleverly-placed subset or add-on, and hope to recover most or all of full fine-tuning’s accuracy at a fraction of the cost.</p>
</section>
</section>

<section id="additive-fine-tuning" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="additive-fine-tuning" id="additive-fine-tuning">Additive Fine-Tuning</h2>
<p>Additive methods leave 100% of the pretrained weights frozen and get their trainable parameters entirely from <em>new</em> modules bolted onto the network. The paper splits these into three groups: adapter-based, soft-prompt-based, and a grab-bag “others” category for methods that don’t fit either mold cleanly.</p>
<section id="adapter-based-fine-tuning" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="adapter-based-fine-tuning" id="adapter-based-fine-tuning">Adapter-based fine-tuning</h3>
<p>The core idea, introduced by the <strong>Sequential Adapter</strong>, is to insert a small bottleneck feed-forward module — a down-projection, a nonlinearity, and an up-projection — into each transformer layer, and train <em>only</em> that module (plus, often, layer norm parameters) while freezing everything else. The paper gives the formula for the adapter transformation as a residual bottleneck:</p>
<p><span class="math display">\[X' = \big(\mathrm{ReLU}(X W_{\text{down}})\big) W_{\text{up}} + X\]</span></p>
<p>where <span class="math inline">\(W_{\text{down}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}\)</span> projects the <span class="math inline">\(d\)</span>-dimensional hidden state down to a small bottleneck dimension <span class="math inline">\(k \ll d\)</span>, and <span class="math inline">\(W_{\text{up}} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times d}\)</span> projects it back up. The final <span class="math inline">\(+X\)</span> is a residual connection, so if the adapter learns to output near-zero, the layer behaves like the untouched pretrained model — a nice property for stable optimization.</p>

<p>From that starting point, the paper walks through a lineage of refinements:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Residual / Parallel Adapter</strong> moves the adapter out of the sequential path and runs it <em>in parallel</em> with the attention or FFN sub-layer instead of after it, which reduces the added inference latency since the adapter computation can overlap with the main computation rather than blocking it.</li>
<li><strong>CoDA</strong> (Conditional Adapter) adds a <em>sparse activation</em> mechanism, so only a subset of tokens actually route through the adapter’s extra computation, cutting the added inference cost further.</li>
<li><strong>AdapterDrop</strong> observes that you don’t need adapters in every layer at inference time — you can drop adapters from lower layers dynamically to speed up inference with a small accuracy trade-off.</li>
<li><strong>Tiny-Attn Adapter</strong> adds a small attention module inside the adapter bottleneck itself, letting the adapter capture token-interaction information that a purely position-wise bottleneck MLP would miss.</li>
<li><strong>AdapterFusion</strong> addresses multi-task settings: train separate adapters per task first, then train a small attention-based fusion layer on top that learns how to combine multiple task adapters’ outputs for a new task — a form of non-destructive knowledge composition.</li>
<li><strong>MerA</strong> merges multiple pretrained adapters into a single adapter via parameter averaging/interpolation, rather than keeping and routing between several of them.</li>
<li><strong>Hyperformer++</strong> uses a shared <em>hypernetwork</em> to generate the adapter weights for each task/layer conditioned on task and layer embeddings, so the number of trainable parameters grows much more slowly as you add more tasks.</li>
<li><strong>AdapterSoup</strong> performs weight averaging (“souping”) across adapters trained on different domains at inference time to improve out-of-domain generalization, without any additional training.</li>
</ul>
</section>
<section id="soft-prompt-based-fine-tuning" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="soft-prompt-based-fine-tuning" id="soft-prompt-based-fine-tuning">Soft prompt-based fine-tuning</h3>
<p>Instead of adding new <em>layers</em>, soft-prompt methods add new <em>trainable tokens</em> (continuous embeddings, not real vocabulary words) that get prepended to the input or to intermediate representations. The model itself stays completely frozen; only these prompt embeddings are trained.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Prompt-tuning</strong> prepends a short sequence of trainable embedding vectors <span class="math inline">\(P\)</span> directly to the input embeddings, so the model sees <span class="math inline">\(\hat X = \mathrm{Concat}(P, X)\)</span>. Nothing else changes — the frozen transformer just runs forward on this concatenated input. Simplicity is the whole point; the price is that at small model scale, prompt-tuning tends to underperform other PEFT methods notably (this shows up starkly in the paper’s own experiments — see <a href="#sec-experiments" class="quarto-xref">Section&nbsp;1.10</a>).</li>
<li><strong>Prefix-tuning</strong> is the more expressive cousin: instead of one prompt inserted at the input layer, it inserts a trainable prefix <em>at every attention layer</em>, directly into the keys and values: <span class="math inline">\(\mathrm{Attn}(XW_q, [\hat P_k, XW_k], [\hat P_v, XW_v])\)</span>. Because it influences every layer’s attention computation rather than just the first layer’s input, prefix-tuning is generally more capable than plain prompt-tuning at a given parameter budget.</li>
</ul>

<ul>
<li><strong>WARP</strong> (“Word-level Adversarial RePrompting”) learns prompt tokens together with a task-specific output/verbalizer layer, framing prompting closer to how masked-language-model prompting is used for classification.</li>
<li><strong>P-tuning</strong> treats the prompt embeddings as free continuous parameters optimized jointly with a small prompt encoder (rather than being tied to actual vocabulary embeddings), improving stability over the naive discrete-prompt-search predecessors it followed.</li>
<li><strong>SPoT</strong> (“Soft Prompt Transfer”) pretrains a soft prompt on one or more <em>source</em> tasks, then uses that as the initialization for a <em>target</em> task’s prompt-tuning, transferring task knowledge purely through the prompt rather than through model weights.</li>
<li><strong>ATTEMPT</strong> extends this transfer idea by learning to attend over and interpolate <em>multiple</em> source-task prompts when initializing/training the target-task prompt, rather than picking just one source.</li>
<li><strong>MPT</strong> (“Multi-task Prompt Tuning”) distills a single shared prompt from a collection of source-task prompts (via a form of low-rank task-shared/task-specific decomposition) and uses that as a strong general starting point across new tasks.</li>
</ul>
</section>
<section id="others" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="others" id="others">Others</h3>
<p>A handful of additive methods don’t fit neatly under “adapter” or “soft prompt”:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>(IA)³</strong> (“Infused Adapter by Inhibiting and Amplifying Inner Activations”) learns a small set of per-channel scaling vectors that element-wise rescale the keys, values, and FFN activations — no bottleneck projections at all, just learned rescaling. It’s strikingly parameter-cheap while remaining competitive on accuracy (see <a href="#sec-experiments" class="quarto-xref">Section&nbsp;1.10</a>).</li>
<li><strong>LST</strong> (“Ladder Side-Tuning”) trains a small separate “ladder” side network that runs alongside the frozen backbone and reads intermediate activations from it, avoiding backpropagation through the large frozen model entirely — which saves <em>activation memory</em>, not just parameter count.</li>
<li><strong>PASTA</strong> modifies special-token representations (like <code>[CLS]</code>/<code>[SEP]</code>-style tokens) directly, adding trainable perturbations there instead of throughout the sequence.</li>
<li><strong>AttentionFusion</strong> learns to combine multiple existing PEFT modules’ outputs via a trainable attention mechanism over them.</li>
<li><strong>Hadamard Adapter</strong> replaces the usual bottleneck matrix multiplication with an element-wise (Hadamard) product against a trainable vector, an even lighter-weight adapter variant.</li>
</ul>
</section>
</section>
<section id="partial-fine-tuning" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="partial-fine-tuning" id="partial-fine-tuning">Partial Fine-Tuning</h2>
<p>Where additive methods add new parameters, partial fine-tuning methods keep the parameter count fixed and instead ask: <strong>which existing parameters actually need to move?</strong> The rest stay frozen at their pretrained values. The paper splits this family into three groups.</p>
<section id="bias-update" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="bias-update" id="bias-update">Bias update</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>BitFit</strong> is the simplest possible partial fine-tuning method: freeze every weight matrix in the network, and train <em>only the bias terms</em> (the <span class="math inline">\(b\)</span> vectors in <span class="math inline">\(XW+b\)</span>, plus the final classification head). Astonishingly, on many GLUE tasks this recovers most of full fine-tuning’s performance while touching well under 0.1% of the parameters.</li>
<li><strong>U/S-BitFit</strong> extends BitFit with unstructured or structured search over <em>which</em> biases matter most, rather than naively training all of them — a lightweight variant selection step on top of the base idea.</li>
</ul>
</section>
<section id="pretrained-weight-masking" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="pretrained-weight-masking" id="pretrained-weight-masking">Pretrained weight masking</h3>
<p>Rather than restricting <em>which type</em> of parameter to train (bias vs.&nbsp;weight), this group restricts <em>which individual weight entries</em> get updated, via a binary mask <span class="math inline">\(M\)</span> applied on top of the original weight <span class="math inline">\(W\)</span>:</p>
<p><span class="math display">\[\hat W = M \odot W\]</span></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Threshold-Mask</strong> builds <span class="math inline">\(M\)</span> by thresholding some importance score per weight, keeping only those above a cutoff <span class="math inline">\(\tau\)</span>: <span class="math inline">\(M = \mathbb{I}[s_{i,j} &gt; \tau]\)</span>.</li>
<li><strong>FISH Mask</strong> uses the <strong>Fisher information</strong> of each parameter (roughly: how sensitive the loss is to perturbing that parameter) as the importance score, keeping the top-<span class="math inline">\(k\)</span> most Fisher-important weights: <span class="math inline">\(M = \mathbb{I}[\text{top-}k(f_{i,j})]\)</span>.</li>
</ul>
</section>
<section id="delta-weight-masking" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="delta-weight-masking" id="delta-weight-masking">Delta weight masking</h3>
<p>A closely related but distinct idea: instead of masking the <em>original</em> weight, compute a full gradient-based update <span class="math inline">\(\Delta W\)</span> as usual, but then mask the update itself before applying it, so only a sparse subset of weights actually change value:</p>
<p><span class="math display">\[\hat W = W + M \odot \Delta W\]</span></p>

<section id="reparameterized-fine-tuning" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="reparameterized-fine-tuning" id="reparameterized-fine-tuning">Reparameterized Fine-Tuning</h2>
<p>This is the family that contains <strong>LoRA</strong>, almost certainly the single most widely-used PEFT method in practice today, and its many descendants. The core idea: “reparameterized fine-tuning methods utilize low-rank transformation to reduce the number of trainable parameters while allowing operating with high-dimensional matrices.” Rather than training the full weight update <span class="math inline">\(\Delta W \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times k}\)</span> directly (which has <span class="math inline">\(d \times k\)</span> trainable numbers), you constrain <span class="math inline">\(\Delta W\)</span> to be expressible via a much smaller number of parameters, train only those, and reconstruct <span class="math inline">\(\Delta W\)</span> from them.</p>
<section id="low-rank-decomposition" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="low-rank-decomposition" id="low-rank-decomposition">Low-rank decomposition</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>Intrinsic SAID</strong> is the conceptual ancestor here: it’s built on the idea of “intrinsic dimensionality” — the observation that fine-tuning a large model often only needs to move it along a surprisingly low-dimensional subspace of its full parameter space. It reparameterizes the update as <span class="math inline">\(\Delta W = F(W_r)\)</span>, where <span class="math inline">\(F\)</span> is a (Fastfood) transform projecting a small trainable vector <span class="math inline">\(W_r \in \mathbb{R}^r\)</span> up into the full parameter space, with <span class="math inline">\(r \ll d\)</span>.</li>
<li><strong>LoRA</strong> (“Low-Rank Adaptation”) makes this idea concrete and practical for transformers specifically: freeze <span class="math inline">\(W\)</span>, and add a trainable update factored as the product of two small matrices,</li>
</ul>
<p><span class="math display">\[\Delta W = W_{\text{down}} W_{\text{up}}, \qquad W_{\text{down}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times r},\ W_{\text{up}} \in \mathbb{R}^{r\times k},\ r \ll \{d,k\}\]</span></p>
<p>Critically, because <span class="math inline">\(\Delta W\)</span> is just an ordinary matrix once multiplied out, it can be <strong>merged back into <span class="math inline">\(W\)</span> after training</strong> (<span class="math inline">\(W' = W + \Delta W\)</span>), meaning LoRA adds <em>zero</em> extra inference latency compared to the original model — a major practical advantage over adapters, which do add a small forward-pass cost.</p>

</ul>

<section id="lora-derivatives" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="lora-derivatives" id="lora-derivatives">LoRA derivatives</h3>
<p>LoRA’s simplicity made it extremely easy to extend, and the paper groups the resulting explosion of follow-up work into five sub-categories.</p>
<p><strong>Low-rank adjustment</strong> — methods that make the rank <span class="math inline">\(r\)</span> itself adaptive rather than a fixed hyperparameter:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>DyLoRA</strong> trains across a <em>range</em> of ranks <span class="math inline">\([r_{\min}, r_{\max}]\)</span> simultaneously by randomly truncating the matrices at different ranks <span class="math inline">\(b\)</span> during training (<span class="math inline">\(W_{\text{down}\downarrow b} = W_{\text{down}}[:b,:]\)</span>, <span class="math inline">\(W_{\text{up}\downarrow b}=W_{\text{up}}[:, :b]\)</span>), producing a single set of weights that works well across many rank settings so you can pick your accuracy/efficiency trade-off <em>after</em> training without retraining.</li>
<li><strong>AdaLoRA</strong> reparameterizes the update via an SVD-like decomposition, <span class="math inline">\(\Delta W = P\Lambda Q\)</span> (with <span class="math inline">\(P,Q\)</span> orthogonal and <span class="math inline">\(\Lambda\)</span> a diagonal matrix of “singular values”), and then <em>prunes</em> the least important singular values during training — effectively letting each weight matrix in the network get a different, learned, importance-driven rank rather than one fixed rank for everything.</li>
<li><strong>IncreLoRA</strong> grows rank incrementally, formalized as <span class="math inline">\(\Delta W = W_{\text{down}}\Lambda W_{\text{up}}\)</span> with <span class="math inline">\(\Lambda = [\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_r]\)</span> arbitrary constants — starting small and adding rank where it’s needed most as training progresses, the inverse strategy of AdaLoRA’s start-big-and-prune approach.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>LoRA-guided pretrained weight update</strong> — methods that use the LoRA factors to guide changes to the <em>original</em> frozen weight itself, rather than only adding a separate low-rank path:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>DeltaLoRA</strong> periodically folds the <em>change</em> in the low-rank product back into the frozen weight during training: <span class="math inline">\(W \leftarrow W + \big(W_{\text{down}}^{(t+1)}W_{\text{up}}^{(t+1)} - W_{\text{down}}^{(t)}W_{\text{up}}^{(t)}\big)\)</span>, effectively letting the “frozen” weight drift slightly, guided by the LoRA update’s trajectory.</li>
<li><strong>LoRAPrune</strong> combines LoRA with structured pruning, applying a group-wise binary mask on top of the combined weight: <span class="math inline">\(\delta = (W + W_{\text{down}}W_{\text{up}}) \odot M\)</span>, <span class="math inline">\(M \in \{0,1\}^{1\times G}\)</span>, so the model gets smaller (via pruning) <em>and</em> cheaper to fine-tune (via LoRA) at once.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Quantization adaptation</strong> — the methods that make LoRA practical at LLM scale by combining it with weight quantization:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>QLoRA</strong> quantizes the frozen backbone weights down to 4-bit NormalFloat precision (plus double quantization of the quantization constants themselves, and paged optimizers to handle memory spikes), while still training ordinary full-precision LoRA adapters on top. This is the method responsible for making it feasible to fine-tune a 65B-parameter model on a single consumer/prosumer GPU, and it shows up prominently in this paper’s own memory-efficiency experiments (<a href="#sec-experiments" class="quarto-xref">Section&nbsp;1.10</a>).</li>
<li><strong>QA-LoRA</strong> uses group-wise quantization, partitioning each weight matrix’s columns into <span class="math inline">\(L\)</span> groups for finer-grained quantization than QLoRA’s per-tensor approach, and is designed so the final merged model stays quantized after training.</li>
<li><strong>LOFTQ</strong> (“LoRA-Fine-Tuning-aware Quantization”) initializes the LoRA factors <em>specifically to compensate for quantization error</em>, solving <span class="math inline">\(\Delta W = \mathrm{SVD}(W - Q_t)\)</span> where <span class="math inline">\(Q_t\)</span> is the quantized weight — instead of quantizing first and hoping LoRA fixes the damage during training, it explicitly initializes LoRA to offset the quantization error from step zero.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>LoRA-based improvements</strong> — miscellaneous refinements to LoRA’s training dynamics or numerical behavior:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Kernel-mix-lite</strong> treats each attention head as a partially independent estimator, mixing a shared LoRA component (across all heads) with small head-specific components.</li>
<li><strong>Laplace-LoRA</strong> applies a Bayesian <strong>Laplace approximation</strong> over the LoRA parameters post-training, which the paper’s Further Directions section flags as valuable for improving the <em>calibration</em> of PEFT-tuned models — i.e., making their confidence scores more trustworthy.</li>
<li><strong>LoRA-FA</strong> (“LoRA with Frozen-A”) decomposes the down-projection matrix via QR decomposition and <em>freezes</em> it, updating only the up-projection: <span class="math inline">\(\Delta W = QRW_{\text{up}}\)</span>. Freezing half the LoRA parameters roughly halves LoRA’s already-small memory footprint with little accuracy cost.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>LoRA-based multi-task fine-tuning</strong> — extending LoRA from single-task to multi-task/transfer settings:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>LoRAHub</strong> composes multiple task-specific LoRA modules by learning a weighted combination: <span class="math inline">\(\hat m = (w_1 W^1_{\text{down}} + \cdots + w_N W^N_{\text{down}})(w_1 W^1_{\text{up}} + \cdots + w_N W^N_{\text{up}})\)</span> — a way to assemble a new-task adapter out of a library of existing task adapters without retraining from scratch.</li>
<li><strong>MoELoRA</strong> combines LoRA with a <strong>mixture-of-experts</strong> gating mechanism, routing different inputs to different LoRA “experts” based on task-motivated gating signals.</li>
<li><strong>L-LoRA</strong> (“Linearized LoRA”) linearizes the fine-tuned model’s behavior via a first-order Taylor expansion around the pretrained parameters: <span class="math inline">\(f_{\theta_0}(x;\phi(t)) \approx f_{\theta_0}(x;\phi(0)) + \nabla_\phi f_{\theta_0}(x;\phi(0))^\top(\phi(t)-\phi(0))\)</span>, which the paper notes is useful specifically for multi-task model-merging scenarios, since linearized models compose more predictably when you try to add or subtract task vectors from each other.</li>
</ul>

<section id="hybrid-fine-tuning" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="hybrid-fine-tuning" id="hybrid-fine-tuning">Hybrid Fine-Tuning</h2>
<p>“Hybrid fine-tuning approaches aim to combine various PEFT approaches, such as adapter, prefix-tuning, and LoRA, to leverage the strengths of each method and mitigate their weaknesses.” Instead of picking one family, hybrid methods mix and match. The paper splits hybrid approaches by <em>how</em> the combination is decided: by a human designer, or by an automated search algorithm.</p>
<section id="manual-combination" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="manual-combination" id="manual-combination">Manual combination</h3>
<ul>
<li><strong>MAM Adapter</strong> (“Mix-And-Match Adapter”) combines a scaled <em>parallel adapter</em> with <em>prefix-tuning</em>, but allocates the budgets asymmetrically: it uses prefix-tuning with a smaller dimension at the attention layers, and gives relatively more trainable parameters to adapters at the FFN layers — a design choice motivated by empirical findings that adapters and prefix-tuning each have a natural “home” (FFN vs.&nbsp;attention) where they’re most effective.</li>
<li><strong>U-MAM / S-MAM</strong> are variants that apply <strong>NAS (Neural Architecture Search)</strong> together with pruning on top of the MAM Adapter idea, automatically deciding <em>which</em> of the mixed components’ parameters need updating rather than fixing that by hand.</li>
<li><strong>Compacter</strong> builds on the adapter idea but replaces the down/up projection matrices with a <strong>parameterized hypercomplex multiplication (PHM)</strong> layer, constructing the weight as a sum of Kronecker products: <span class="math inline">\(W = \sum_i A_i \otimes (s_i t_i^\top)\)</span>. This lets Compacter share structure across the projection in a way that needs even fewer trainable parameters than a standard adapter bottleneck.</li>
<li><strong>UniPELT</strong> goes further and incorporates <em>three</em> PEFT types at once — sequential adapter, prefix-tuning, and LoRA — inside a single layer, with a learned <strong>gating mechanism</strong> that dynamically decides, per input, how much weight to give each submodule’s contribution.</li>
</ul>
</section>
<section id="automatic-combination" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="automatic-combination" id="automatic-combination">Automatic combination</h3>
<p>Rather than a human deciding the mixture recipe, these methods search for it:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>AutoPEFT</strong> uses <strong>Bayesian optimization</strong> to search over an architecture space that includes sequential adapters, parallel adapters, and prefix-tuning, automatically discovering which combination (and where in the network) works best for a given task/budget.</li>
<li><strong>S³Delta-M</strong> performs a <strong>differentiable structure search</strong> over delta-tuning configurations, combining LoRA, Compacter, BitFit, and LNFit as candidate building blocks across layers, with explicit control over the resulting sparsity.</li>
<li><strong>S⁴</strong> searches over four axes at once: “layer groupings, trainable parameter allocations, tunable groups, and PEFT module assignments,” dividing the network’s layers into four groups that can each receive a distinct PEFT configuration rather than applying one uniform recipe network-wide.</li>
</ul>
</section>
</section>
<section id="unified-fine-tuning" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="unified-fine-tuning" id="unified-fine-tuning">Unified Fine-Tuning</h2>
<p>Unified approaches take a step back from “combine multiple existing methods” and instead try to design <strong>one general framework</strong> that many specific PEFT configurations fall out of as special cases — providing “a unified framework for fine-tuning, which streamlines the incorporation of diverse fine-tuning methods into a cohesive architecture, ensuring consistency and efficiency.”</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>AdaMix</strong> treats each adaptation module (e.g., each adapter) as an individual “expert” and uses <strong>stochastic routing</strong> to select which down-projection/up-projection pairing gets used on a given forward pass, then applies consistency regularization so the multiple experts behave coherently at inference time (where routing is typically averaged rather than stochastic).</li>
<li><strong>SparseAdapter</strong> applies standard <strong>network pruning</strong> techniques to build a single framework compatible with both adapters and LoRA — it assigns an importance score to each parameter and removes (zeros out) the least important ones past a threshold, unifying pruning-style sparsification with either additive or reparameterized PEFT.</li>
<li><strong>ProPETL</strong> (“Prototype PEFT Learning”) shares <strong>a single prototype network across layers and tasks</strong>, then uses binary masks to carve out layer-specific and task-specific sub-networks from that one shared prototype — so instead of training separate PEFT parameters per layer or per task from scratch, most of the capacity is shared and only the masks differ. Formally it optimizes <span class="math inline">\(\max \sum_i \log P(Y_i \mid X_i;\ \theta_{lm}, \theta_{sub})\)</span>, where <span class="math inline">\(\theta_{sub}\)</span> denotes the shared prototype’s sub-network parameters. This method reappears later as one of the paper’s own 11 experimentally-tested PEFT methods (as “ProPELT” — sequential-adapter, prefix, and LoRA variants of the same prototype idea).</li>
</ul>
</section>
<section id="sec-experiments" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="sec-experiments" id="sec-experiments">Experiments: How Well Do These Methods Actually Work?</h2>
<p>Reading about 45 methods in the abstract is one thing; the paper backs it up with real experiments. This is arguably the most practically useful section for a practitioner deciding <em>which</em> PEFT method to reach for.</p>
<section id="setup" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="setup" id="setup">Setup</h3>

That’s a deliberately broad slice — one or more representatives from additive, partial, reparameterized, and hybrid/unified families each.</p>

<section id="fine-tuning-performance-and-parameter-efficiency" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="fine-tuning-performance-and-parameter-efficiency" id="fine-tuning-performance-and-parameter-efficiency">Fine-tuning performance and parameter efficiency</h3>
<p><strong>On RoBERTa/GLUE (Table III in the original):</strong> Full fine-tuning (FT) is the 100%-parameter baseline (124.6M params for base, 355.3M for large). Some standout results:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>ProPELT<span class="math inline">\(_{\text{Adapter}}\)</span></strong> was the strongest performer overall, using only about 1.5% of full fine-tuning’s trainable parameters while <em>exceeding</em> full fine-tuning’s average GLUE score by roughly 1.3 points on RoBERTa-base and 1.65 points on RoBERTa-large — a rare case of a PEFT method clearly beating the full-parameter baseline, not just matching it.</li>
<li><strong>Prompt-tuning</strong> was the weakest performer by a wide margin — despite using the fewest parameters of any method tested (0.61M on base), its average score came in roughly 10 points below full fine-tuning, and it fell apart particularly badly on RTE (a small, hard entailment task): 58.12 vs.&nbsp;full fine-tuning’s 72.20 on RoBERTa-base.</li>
<li><strong>BitFit</strong>, despite only training bias terms, was competitive with — and on RoBERTa-large actually slightly ahead of — several much-more-parameter-hungry methods (88.57 avg vs.&nbsp;AdapterS’s 88.58 avg on RoBERTa-large, at a fraction of the parameter count: 1.32M vs.&nbsp;19.77M).</li>
<li><strong>AdapterS</strong> (sequential adapter) needed considerably more parameters than most other methods (7.41M on base, 19.77M on large) but delivered correspondingly strong results.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>On T5/WMT16 En-Ro (Table IV):</strong> (IA)³ was the standout efficiency story — 27.58 BLEU on T5-base with only 0.07M trainable parameters, essentially matching full fine-tuning’s 27.42 BLEU (achieved with 222.9M parameters) using roughly 1/3000th the trainable parameter count. LoRA scored slightly higher still (27.78 BLEU) with 0.88M parameters. On T5-large the gap between all methods and full fine-tuning essentially closed entirely (28.12–28.13 BLEU across FT, (IA)³, and LoRA).</p>
<p><strong>On LLaMA/MMLU (Table VI):</strong> Full fine-tuning of LLaMA-7B-Alpaca reached 41.79% 5-shot MMLU accuracy (interestingly, the paper’s Table V shows this number is <em>very</em> sensitive to learning rate: 25.71% at lr=2e-4, 26.65% at lr=5e-5, and 41.79% at lr=1e-6 — a reminder that full fine-tuning at LLM scale needs careful, conservative learning-rate tuning to avoid catastrophic forgetting). Against that baseline:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>LoRA</strong> reached 40.67% with 159.9M trainable parameters (2.32% of the model).</li>
<li><strong>QLoRA</strong> reached 39.96% — close to LoRA — while training a comparable <em>number</em> of LoRA parameters (79.9M, since QLoRA’s LoRA adapters are similarly sized) but, crucially, needing far less memory to do it (see below). The paper summarizes: “QLoRA used half the number of trainable parameters of LoRA but achieves comparable performance.”</li>
<li><strong>(IA)³</strong> was the leanest by far (1.58M params, 0.02% of the model) but paid for it in accuracy — 37.88%, noticeably behind LoRA/QLoRA at this scale.</li>
</ul>

<section id="memory-efficiency" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="memory-efficiency" id="memory-efficiency">Memory efficiency</h3>
<p>This is where the practical payoff of PEFT becomes unambiguous — Table VII in the original reports peak GPU memory usage across every model and method:</p>
<ul>
<li>On <strong>RoBERTa-base</strong>, full fine-tuning used 5.38GB. Curiously, <strong>AdapterS actually used <em>more</em></strong> memory than full fine-tuning here (15.29GB) — inserting adapter modules adds activation memory that, on this small model, ends up outweighing the savings from not storing full-model optimizer states. (IA)³ was the leanest at 2.62GB.</li>
<li>On <strong>LLaMA-7B-Alpaca</strong>, the story flips decisively in PEFT’s favor at scale: full fine-tuning required 169.36GB, while <strong>QLoRA required only 56.46GB</strong> — “QLoRA dramatically reduces GPU memory consumption,” fine-tuning “the LLaMA-7B requiring only 1/3 of the memory required for full fine-tuning.”</li>
<li>On <strong>LLaMA-13B-Alpaca</strong>, the effect is even larger: full fine-tuning needed 287.79GB, while QLoRA needed just 66.60GB — “fine-tuning the LLaMA-13B requiring less than 1/4” of full fine-tuning’s memory. Put as relative reductions: “compared with full fine-tuning, IA3, LoRA, and QLoRA reduce memory usage by 33.55%, 39.46%, and 76.86%” respectively at the 13B scale.</li>
</ul>
<p>The clear headline finding: <strong>memory savings from PEFT scale up dramatically with model size.</strong> At RoBERTa-base scale, some PEFT methods barely help (or even hurt) memory usage; at LLaMA-13B scale, QLoRA cuts memory requirements by more than 75%. If you’re deciding whether PEFT is “worth it” for your use case, model scale is the single biggest factor.</p>
</section>
</section>
<section id="applications-of-peft" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="applications-of-peft" id="applications-of-peft">Applications of PEFT</h2>
<p>Beyond plain single-task fine-tuning, the paper surveys three application areas where PEFT’s modularity turns out to be independently useful.</p>
<section id="multi-task-learning" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="multi-task-learning" id="multi-task-learning">Multi-task learning</h3>
<p>“Multi-task learning is a method that involves training a model on multiple related tasks and exploiting the information shared and transferred between them to improve the performance of each task.” PEFT modules are a natural fit here because they’re small, swappable, and composable: “adapters, prompt-tuning, and LoRA utilize additional modules that can be plugged into PLMs and thus can be used for task-specific fine-tuning to improve generalization of multi-task learning.”</p>
<p>Concretely, the survey describes a few recurring strategies: prompt-tuning methods either “utilize pretrained soft prompts from multiple source tasks to initialize the soft prompt of the target task, based on the similarity between the source and target tasks,” or alternatively “employ multi-task data to learn a single shared prompt and transfer it to the target task” (this is exactly the SPoT / MPT idea, applied). Similarly, “a composition of multiple task-specific LoRA modules is also leveraged to transfer knowledge to new tasks” — a nod to LoRAHub. There’s also a more exotic technique using “arithmetic operators, such as the addition and negation operators, to merge parameters of various PEFT methods trained on different tasks” — literally adding or subtracting task-specific PEFT weight deltas as vectors to compose or remove capabilities.</p>
</section>
<section id="cross-lingual-transfer" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="cross-lingual-transfer" id="cross-lingual-transfer">Cross-lingual transfer</h3>
<p>“Cross-lingual transfer involves transferring knowledge or models from one language to another. Numerous works have employed PEFT methods, such as adapters, for cross-lingual transfer due to their unique modular design.” Because an adapter is a self-contained, swappable module, you can train language-specific and task-specific adapters <em>separately</em> and combine them at inference time for a language/task pair that was never jointly trained on.</p>
<p>Examples the survey highlights: using “sequential adapter to fine-tune and restore the performance of a multilingual neural machine translation model on high-resource languages,” and using sequential adapters “to transfer a pretrained monolingual model to an unseen language.” The most elaborate example is <strong>MAD-X</strong>, which employs “language-specific, task-specific, and invertible adapter to learn language-specific and task-specific transformations” — stacking three different adapter types to handle vocabulary mismatches across languages. <strong>MAD-G</strong> takes this further by <em>generating</em> language adapters on the fly “from language representations based on typological features,” rather than training a fixed adapter per language. There’s also work applying sparse fine-tuning to “train the model on the source language and learn task-specific sparse difference vectors for cross-lingual transfer,” and training “a bilingual language-pair adapter on both the source and target languages for zero-shot cross-lingual transfer.”</p>
</section>
<section id="backdoor-attacks-and-defense" class="level3">
<h3 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="backdoor-attacks-and-defense" id="backdoor-attacks-and-defense">Backdoor attacks and defense</h3>
<p>This is the section that reframes PEFT’s efficiency as a <strong>double-edged sword</strong> from a security standpoint. “Backdoor attacks pose a significant security threat, where a small portion of training samples are contaminated with malicious backdoor triggers,” causing a model to “behave normally on benign samples but predict attacker-selected labels on samples containing the predefined triggers.”</p>
<p>The survey notes research going both directions:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>PEFT as an attack vector:</strong> researchers have used “PEFT methods to construct backdoor attacks, in which backdoor attacks are directly injected into PEFT modules” — since a PEFT module (like an adapter or LoRA weights) is small, portable, and often distributed/shared independently of the base model (e.g., on model hubs), it’s a plausible vehicle for smuggling a backdoor into a downstream deployment.</li>
<li><strong>PEFT as a defense:</strong> conversely, “PEFT can serve as a backdoor defense solution by reducing the model capacity via optimizing only a small number of parameters” — the idea being that a backdoor typically needs enough free capacity to memorize the trigger-to-target-label mapping, and PEFT’s restricted capacity can make that harder to fit. Related work confirms “PEFT can slightly weaken the backdoor attacks,” while at the same time other work has designed “a novel trojan attack for the PEFT paradigm” specifically — so this is very much an open, actively-contested area rather than a settled one in either direction.</li>
</ul>
</section>
</section>
<section id="open-problems-and-further-directions" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="open-problems-and-further-directions" id="open-problems-and-further-directions">Open Problems and Further Directions</h2>
<p>The paper closes its technical content with five concrete directions it thinks the field should pursue next:</p>
<ol type="1">
<li><strong>Lightweight hybrid PEFT methods.</strong> Existing hybrid methods have mostly combined a narrow set of building blocks — “exploration has been limited to PEFT methods such as adapter, LoRA, prefix-tuning, and BitFit.” The authors want to see hybrid designs that “leverage multiple PEFT methods to improve performance while minimizing the number of trainable parameters,” pointing out that current hybrid approaches typically <em>increase</em> both parameter count and memory usage relative to a single PEFT method, even as they improve accuracy — the efficiency-vs-performance trade-off hasn’t actually been solved by hybridization yet, just shifted.</li>
<li><strong>More LoRA-derived methods, especially around pruning and quantization.</strong> Given how active this sub-area already is, the authors specifically flag “pruning technology and weight quantification” as the most promising directions for further reducing LoRA’s already-small footprint, particularly for LLM-scale deployment.</li>
<li><strong>Better PEFT tooling/libraries.</strong> While libraries like Hugging Face’s <code>peft</code> and AdapterHub already exist, “not all PEFT methods are currently integrated into these two libraries” — many of the methods cataloged in this survey (especially the newer hybrid/unified ones) don’t yet have accessible, production-ready implementations, which is a real barrier to adoption and fair comparison.</li>
<li><strong>Explainability.</strong> Despite the sheer number of PEFT methods proposed, “there is a lack of comprehensive studies exploring the reasons behind their ability to achieve comparable performance and reduce trainable parameters” — i.e., we mostly know PEFT <em>works</em> empirically, but the theoretical <em>why</em> (intrinsic dimensionality arguments aside) is still thin.</li>
<li><strong>Computer vision and multimodal learning.</strong> PEFT research has concentrated almost entirely on NLP; the authors note “there is still significant room for further exploration and exploitation” of PEFT ideas in vision and multimodal (cross-modality) settings, where transformer backbones are now just as dominant as they are in NLP.</li>
</ol>
</section>
<section id="conclusions" class="level2">
<h2 class="anchored" data-anchor-id="conclusions" id="conclusions">Conclusions</h2>
<p>The paper’s own summary, paraphrased: this survey delivers a structured, five-category taxonomy (additive, partial, reparameterized, hybrid, unified) covering the PEFT literature, walks through the specific mechanics of dozens of methods within that taxonomy, and backs it up with original experiments across three model architectures (RoBERTa, T5, LLaMA) showing that most PEFT methods achieve “comparable or even better performance compared to full fine-tuning” while using a small fraction of the trainable parameters — and, at LLM scale particularly, dramatically less GPU memory, with quantization-aware methods like QLoRA showing the largest gains. The survey also documents PEFT’s reach beyond single-task fine-tuning into multi-task learning, cross-lingual transfer, and (both offensively and defensively) model security. Looking ahead, the authors frame the central open challenge plainly: “there is a clear need to develop PEFT methods that can effectively reduce computational resource demands and memory usage during fine-tuning” as models keep growing — and position the survey itself as providing “a bird’s-eye view of PEFT methods for PLMs and inspiring further research in this area.”</p>
</section>
